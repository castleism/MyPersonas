White, green, yellow, oolong, black, and dark tea can look like six unrelated worlds. They begin with one species: Camellia sinensis.

What changes is the leaf's path. A maker may wither it, heat it, bruise or roll it, allow more or less oxidation, shape it, dry it, roast it, or guide a microbial transformation. Cultivar, place, season, harvest, storage, and brewing then add more variation.

If tea terminology has ever felt like a wall of names, begin here: identify the plant, then ask what happened to the leaf.

Tea and herbal infusion are both welcome at this table. They are not the same category.

Tea comes from Camellia sinensis. White, green, yellow, oolong, black, and dark tea are shaped by different plant material, places, harvests, processing choices, storage, and brewing.

Peppermint, rooibos, chamomile, and chaga come from other plants or fungi. “Tisane” or “herbal infusion” is the more precise umbrella term for those cups.

That distinction is not a purity test, and it does not make one drink better. It helps us ask better questions: What organism is this? Which part is used? How was it prepared? What evidence supports a historical, sensory, or health-related statement? Are there safety or medication considerations that require a qualified source?

Being precise lets every ingredient keep its own story.

### Instagram post 04

The dry leaf tells only the first half of the story.

After brewing, open one leaf and look: bud, stem, edge, color, tears, evenness. Do not ask whether it is universally beautiful. Ask whether it behaves like the style and processing it claims to be.

One small practice: photograph the dry and wet leaf from the same tea. What changed?

### Facebook post 04

The dry leaf gives you clues. The opened leaf gives you another set.

After brewing, let the leaf cool enough to handle safely. Open it gently and look for the plucking standard, buds, stems, torn or intact edges, color changes, thickness, and evenness. Compare those details with the dry leaf and with the tea's stated style.

This is not a universal beauty contest. Broken leaf can be intentional. Small particles infuse differently from large intact leaves. Different categories, regions, and production goals create different visual results.

The useful question is not “Does this look expensive?” It is “What does this leaf help me understand about how the tea was made and how it behaved in the cup?”

Try a two-photo record today: dry leaf before brewing, opened leaf after brewing. Add one sentence about what changed.

### Facebook post 06

Try Being Tea Co.'s five-pass practice before deciding whether a tea is “good.”

**Look.** Notice color, clarity, leaf, and the way the liquor moves.

**Smell.** Compare the dry leaf, warmed leaf, liquor, and empty cup if you can.

**Sip.** Notice the first impression without racing to name a flavor.

**Feel.** Attend to texture, weight, astringency, warmth, cooling, and how the cup sits in the mouth.

**Wait.** Notice the finish and what remains after the swallow.

Write one honest word for each pass. Your description does not need to match a tasting wheel or a seller's notes. The goal is not to perform expertise. It is to build a record of attention that becomes more useful over time.

Save the five-pass practice and use it with the next tea you already own.

### Facebook post 07

One leaf. One water. Three brews.

Set up three small cups with the same tea and water. Keep the vessel and leaf-to-water ratio steady.

- Cup A: use a lower temperature than your baseline.
- Cup B: use your normal temperature.
- Cup C: use a higher temperature than your baseline.

Keep time and every other variable steady. Compare aroma, bitterness, astringency, texture, balance, and finish. Record what changed. Do not hunt for a universal winner; choose the cup that feels most coherent to you, then decide which variable to test next. On another day, test lower/baseline/higher time while keeping temperature fixed.

Time and temperature affect extraction, but they work together with particle size, leaf ratio, water chemistry, and the tea itself. This is why a chart can offer a starting point but cannot taste on your behalf.

A disappointing steep is data, not defeat.

### Facebook post 08

Four practical variables are a useful starting point for a cup:

**Leaf-to-water ratio.** More leaf changes the concentration and often changes the timing that makes sense.

**Water.** Mineral composition and pH can alter color, volatile aroma, bitterness/astringency, and overall taste.

**Temperature.** Hotter water generally extracts soluble material more quickly, but “delicate” does not automatically mean cold and “dark” does not automatically mean boiling.

**Time.** Time works with every variable above. Small particles, compressed leaf, and repeated infusions behave differently.

Change all four at once and you may improve the tea without learning why. Change one variable, record the result, and repeat.

Maker guidance is a useful starting point—not a commandment. Your goal is a repeatable cup and a clear reason for the next adjustment.

### Facebook post 09

Tea is mostly water in the cup, so water is not a neutral background. It is an ingredient.

Try a simple comparison with a tea you already know:

1. Choose potable tap water known to be safe to drink, filtered water, and one moderate-mineral bottled water.
2. Use the same tea, leaf mass, vessel, temperature, and steeping time for all three.
3. Taste blind if possible.
4. Compare aroma, color, brightness, bitterness, astringency, texture, and finish.
5. Record which water worked best with this tea—not which water is universally superior.

Research shows that water chemistry can change green tea's color, overall taste, and volatile aroma profile. The practical goal is compatibility, repeatability, and a result you enjoy—not a universal winner.

You do not need a collector's notebook or a perfect tasting vocabulary. Record enough to reproduce the cup. Then write one honest result and one next change.

For example, the next change might be shorter time, cooler water, more leaf, a different water, or no change at all. The point is not to optimize every moment. It is to notice why a cup worked and to make learning cumulative.

After several sessions with the same tea, your own record may become more useful for that tea than a generic chart. Calm does not require the uncertainty to disappear. It can begin with a method.

Save the line, use it with a tea you already own, and bring one observation back next week.
